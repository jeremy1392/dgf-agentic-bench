\begin{abstract}
Can tool-using language models perform governance decisions normally assigned to human
project reviewers? DGF-Bench contains 300 synthetic projects with documents, executable
policies, authoritative structured facts, and scored review contracts. Across 899 evaluable
model--project runs, strict gate success is 94.98\% for Gemini 3.8 Flash, 83.29\% for GPT-5.6 Luna,
and 74.18\% for DeepSeek v4.1 Flash; complete-route success is 76.92\%, 42.33\%, and 24.67\%.
A deterministic control executes the supplied rules and succeeds on all 1,700 gates: this
information condition establishes no incremental language-model advantage. We audit all 690
gates with failed evidence components. A post-hoc lexical-or-structural provenance criterion,
which accepts exact field-value subsets despite field order or equivalent numeric serialization,
yields gate success of 99.06\%,
85.53\%, and 77.24\%. These sensitivity results are not semantic adjudication. Decision confusion
matrices and procurement diagnostics distinguish representation defects from substantive errors.
A follow-up completes 135 runs on 15 existing dossiers; only nine, three, and zero dossiers,
respectively, pass all three trajectories under the original strict criterion. An offline counterexample produces identical text
in 26 Word documents despite different required decisions, showing why removing structured
facts requires an information-sufficiency check. Total recorded API expenditure is USD~99.58.
We release sources, documents, traces, unchanged primary scores, and analyses. The results
characterize scaffolded rule application and evidence reliability; they motivate matched-input
extraction studies rather than establish workforce effects.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} governance reviews; AI agents; rule application; evidence attribution; benchmark; repeatability.
