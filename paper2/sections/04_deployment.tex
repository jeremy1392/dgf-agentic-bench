\section{The role of forward deployed engineers}
\label{sec:deployment}

Here, a forward deployed engineer (FDE) denotes an engineer working with an organization's
domain owners to integrate and operate a review system. The contribution of the role is not
assumed from its title. It consists of deliverables that make a review contract executable,
observable, and maintainable. The benchmark already supplies many of these prerequisites;
in an enterprise they must be built and kept correct.

\subsection{From a generated contract to an operational service}

The first task is policy formalization. A specialist and the policy owner must agree which
decision conditions are mandatory, which permit an exception, and which require interpretation.
The implementation should record version, scope, effective date, and responsible authority for
each rule. A model's explanation is not a substitute for resolving contradictory policies.
Ambiguities discovered during formalization are findings about the organization's process and
should remain visible in the deployment record.

The second task is evidence integration. Synthetic snapshots make facts convenient to read;
real evidence comes from versioned repositories, identity services, ticketing systems, monitoring,
contracts, and people. Each connector needs access control, freshness checks, stable source
identifiers, and recorded observations. If two sources disagree, the system needs an escalation
or precedence rule rather than silently selecting whichever supports approval. The cost of
producing a reliable snapshot belongs in the deployment, even if the subsequent model review
becomes almost trivial.

The third task is authority. Recommendation, conditional approval, and execution are distinct
capabilities. The reviewer must act within a versioned mandate whose scope includes the project,
action, and any conditions. An exception mechanism must retain the unresolved finding and identify
who accepts it. Approval status must not be inferred solely from a fluent rationale. The benchmark's
rejected conditional-approval attempts make this distinction concrete: a proposed call can be
invalid even when a later submitted decision is correct.

The fourth task is a shared case state. Later gates need to know which obligations remain open,
which evidence is stale, and which prior opinions have been superseded. Passing an unstructured
summary alone can lose conditions; copying every historical record can create contradictions.
An implementation should retain typed decisions, evidence references, policy versions, unresolved
obligations, and the causal relationship between reviews. Gates can be introduced incrementally
while using the same state and interface design.

\subsection{Failure-informed engineering priorities}

The observed failure classes suggest testable interventions. An evidence serializer could attach
verbatim spans and stable source identifiers directly from the read log, avoiding a model's
reconstruction of exact quotations. A consistency check could flag a submitted approval whose
rationale explicitly asks for rework. A finalization contract could require abstention or
escalation when resource limits prevent a supported answer, instead of permitting a placeholder
approval. A handoff validator could ensure that an unresolved upstream prohibition cannot
silently disappear from the final review.

These interventions could reduce particular failures, but their effects are not measured here.
Each should be evaluated as a new system version on held-out cases, recording any new failure
modes and human burden. A validator that catches an error but routes every case to a specialist
may improve decision safety without reducing labor. A serializer that makes quotes exact may
raise strict CSR without improving substantive judgment. The outcome attributed to an
intervention should match what it changes.

Operational performance is a property of the complete system: policy owners, evidence systems,
model, validators, authorization controls, and exception handling. The FDE's recurring work is
part of that system. Initial integration hours are a transition investment; repeated policy
changes, connector repairs, evaluations, incident investigations, and model migrations are
recurring work. Moving them into an external supplier does not remove them from the DGF labor
boundary. Separate reporting of build effort and recurring effort enables both an annual
capacity estimate and a cumulative payback analysis.

\subsection{Testing the implementation hypothesis}

We hypothesize that a connected implementation with shared evidence and obligations can avoid
some re-reading, re-entry, and reconciliation across review teams. The relevant comparison is
with locally optimized gate tools at comparable project mix, authority, quality, and automation
scope. Deployment can remain incremental in both conditions. Measure duplicated evidence
collection, downstream correction, specialist review hours, recurring support, and total
quality-constrained human work. Assign implementation approaches across comparable workflows
where feasible, or use a staged comparison with explicit confounding controls.

An improvement would support the value of the shared implementation, not prove that a particular
job title caused it. No FDE deployment is included in the current experiment. Likewise, the idea
that governance will automate before other business functions is a comparative hypothesis: it
requires matched functions, comparable outcome definitions, and observed deployment times.
The present results do not establish a universal ordering. They identify a concrete candidate
for further study because much of the review contract can be specified and inspected.
