\section{Introduction: a gate is a contract, not a profession}

A proposed enterprise project is examined by several groups before it proceeds. A legal reviewer
checks contractual prerequisites; an architecture reviewer checks the technical design; security
and readiness reviewers check protections and operational evidence. Each review consumes
information and produces a decision with reasons, conditions, and obligations. The institutional
label of the reviewer does not determine whether this transformation must always be performed
by a person. The useful unit of analysis is the review contract: the inputs, rule, output,
evidence, and authority that make a particular review acceptable.

This observation motivates a task-substitution hypothesis. If software can satisfy a review
contract and its operational assurance requirements, it can replace some of the human execution
of that review. If it does so across a connected process while reducing total human work, the
same volume of governed projects may require fewer staff. Neither implication requires
abolishing the gate or lowering its standard. Nor does successful execution of a synthetic
contract establish that every enterprise contract has already been captured.

The empirical question in this paper is specific: how reliably do three model endpoints apply
explicit governance rules to synthetic dossiers, investigate with tools, produce evidence-bearing
decisions, and continue through a sequence of gates? The experiment provides a useful early
measurement because it exposes what an agent must return and retains what it actually read and
did. It is also deliberately scaffolded: decision-relevant structured facts and policy rules are
available to the model. Success is evidence about this policy-application task, rather than a
direct comparison with unaided professional judgment.

\subsection{Contributions and claim structure}

The paper has three contributions. First, it documents a released benchmark with 300 distinct
projects, 899 evaluable model--project runs, component-level scores, paired comparisons, and
recorded costs. Second, it separates decision agreement, strict evidence conformity, and
whole-route success, including concrete examples where their interpretations differ. Third, it
defines an implementation and measurement agenda linking task automation to human work: the
engineering artifacts an FDE must build, a minimal labor account, and follow-up studies that
could substantiate or reject the workforce hypothesis.

The second and third levels are research hypotheses. We do not call eventual complete
automation a law: without a deadline or a specified comparison population, that endpoint cannot
be rejected by a finite observation period. The useful empirical target is a change in required
human work over a stated period with quality preserved. A substantial reduction can occur while
some human tasks remain necessary.

\subsection{Relation to prior research and to the companion manuscript}

DGF-Bench belongs to a family of agent evaluations with different task and information
contracts. WorkArena evaluates interactions with enterprise software \citep{drouin2024};
ITBench evaluates operational IT scenarios across SRE, compliance/security, and FinOps
\citep{itbench2025}. $\tau$-bench combines policy-following, domain tools, simulated users,
and evaluation of final database state, and explicitly measures reliability across repeated
trials \citep{taubench2024}. CI-Work examines whether enterprise agents preserve contextual
integrity while conveying useful information \citep{ciwork2026}. AgentDojo evaluates tool
use over untrusted data and prompt injection \citep{debenedetti2024}.

The agentic BPM research agenda concerns process-level integration of agents, including planning,
monitoring, and escalation \citep{agenticbpm2026}. This paper contributes a small, inspectable
review-workflow measurement within that agenda, not evidence that an entire business process
has been automated. Its distinctive feature is the connected review contract and auditable
artifact collection. The deterministic control reported below shows why high model scores
on this particular contract should not be treated as evidence of uniquely linguistic capability.

The workload interpretation follows a task-level distinction between automation and aggregate
employment: displacement of particular tasks can coexist with new tasks and changing demand
\citep{acemoglu2019}. Bainbridge's analysis of automation motivates explicit attention to the
work left for human operators \citep{bainbridge1983}. Field evidence such as
\citet{brynjolfsson2025} illustrates a different empirical object: productivity observed in an
operating organization. We do not transfer a productivity effect from another occupation to DGF
reviewers or infer employment effects from model scores.

This is a shorter, empirical companion to \emph{The Last Human Gate: Forward Deployed Engineering
and the Automation of Enterprise Governance}, by the same author. Both manuscripts use the same
September 2026 model experiment; they are not independent replications. This revision adds a
deterministic control and a structural audit of saved evidence, with no new model trials yet. This paper removes
the universal-law framing, reduces the scenario apparatus, and makes the validation gap an
operational research plan. The original manuscript and data remain available separately.

The routes are author-designed synthetic workflows informed by practitioner experience. Their
definitions, generated artifacts, rule implementation, and scoring interface are public, so
the benchmark specification can be inspected without access to an unpublished practitioner
document. This makes the implemented task inspectable; it does not establish that its rules
represent a particular enterprise or an independently validated professional standard.
