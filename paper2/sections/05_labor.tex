\section{From task success to required human work}
\label{sec:labor}

\subsection{A minimal accounting identity}

Automating a review can remove reading, comparison, documentation, and coordination work. It
can also add exception handling, monitoring, correction, and maintenance. To determine the net
effect, first define the same organizational boundary and project volume in both regimes.
Let $V$ be reviews per measurement period and $h_0$ the baseline mean human hours per review.
Let $p$ be the fraction routed to substantive human exception handling, $h_E$ the total human
hours on each such review, $h_S$ the human hours on a standard-path review, and $h_W$ additional
rework averaged over all reviews. Let $B_0$ and $B_1$ denote case-independent support hours before
and after deployment. Then
\begin{align}
L_0 &= Vh_0+B_0,\\
L_1 &= V\{ph_E+(1-p)h_S+h_W\}+B_1,\label{eq:labor}\quad
N_j=L_j/K\quad (j=0,1).
\end{align}
Here $K$ is a fixed useful-hours convention per FTE per period. The same convention is used
in both regimes. Exception time includes any preliminary review of those same cases. Rework
excludes activities already counted in exception time, ordinary review, or separately counted
repeat visits. Support includes internal and external work within the registered function.

Equation~\eqref{eq:labor} is accounting, not an estimated response to AI adoption. With consistent
inputs it is equivalent to adding the underlying activity hours directly. We make no hypothesis
that it outperforms an algebraically identical comparator. Its purpose is to expose omissions
and make an empirical claim about savings auditable. Gate success, approval rate, and the share
of tool calls completed are not substitutes for any of these human-time variables.

\subsection{Why remaining cases can retain disproportionate work}

Let $w$ denote the exception group's share of baseline case-handling hours, under a fixed
partition of the same case population. Let $a$ be its post-deployment effort divided by its own
baseline effort, and $u$ the corresponding ratio for the standard group. Put
$r=h_W/h_0$ and $b_j=B_j/(Vh_0)$. For positive baseline group means,
\begin{equation}
R:=\frac{N_1}{N_0}=\frac{wa+(1-w)u+r+b_1}{1+b_0}.\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
This is the same labor decomposition as the companion manuscript's
$\phi\eta+(1-\phi)\mu+r+b_A$ numerator, with a smaller, non-reused notation set.
All quantities are defined once in Appendix~\ref{app:notation}. If a group's baseline mean is
zero, use the hours identity instead of an undefined effort ratio.

The exception case share $p$ and its baseline labor share $w$ need not coincide. A small group
of difficult projects may retain much of the original work. The relation follows from the
covariance of routing and baseline effort; Appendix~\ref{app:selection} gives the short proof.
This is a property of selection, not evidence that the benchmark's failing cases are the costly
enterprise cases. Human timing data are needed to establish that relationship.

The difference in the table is produced by the stated assumptions. It is not a discovered
effect, a fitted forecast, or an estimate obtained from the model success rates. Its use is
to show exactly what would need to be measured: assistance on exceptions, residual standard
review, additional rework, and recurring support. The long manuscript's multiple workforce
scenarios are omitted here because repeating such arithmetic does not add empirical evidence.

\subsection{Implications for staffing and employment}

If validated review automation lowers $L_1$ at comparable output and quality, fewer
workload-equivalent FTE are required for those activities. This is a mechanism through which
work previously performed by employees can be replaced. A company may then reduce staffing,
avoid new hires, redeploy people, or increase reviewed volume. Those are observable choices,
not identical outcomes. Required hours, allocated staffing, headcount, and hiring should be
reported separately so that the workforce claim can be evaluated rather than inferred from
a technology demonstration.

An organization could reduce headcount while leaving unmet review demand or transferring
unpaid work to another team. Conversely, it could preserve headcount while governing more projects
with the same effort. The primary comparison therefore standardizes volume and case mix,
retains quality and service requirements, and includes shared supplier support. Initial
implementation effort should also be reported for the investment decision, even when the
primary endpoint concerns recurring annual work.

The benchmark supports the technical premise that models can execute many represented tasks.
It contains no human hours and cannot estimate $R$. The author's projection of an 80\% reduction
in required DGF FTE by 2033 is a hypothesis about a possible deployment outcome, rather than
the numerical complement of any model's accuracy. The next section makes its measurement
conditions and calendar explicit.
